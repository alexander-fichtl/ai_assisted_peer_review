Q1 (Summary):
Briefly summarize the paper and its contributions. Do not critique the paper here. The authors should agree with a well-written summary. Typical range: 3-8 sentences.
The paper proposes NEXCO, a diffusion-based neural solver that makes Adaptive Expansion native to the generative process for combinatorial optimization. It introduces a CO-specific masked corruption that only drops active entries (1 → 0), a time-agnostic GNN denoiser trained with a time-agnostic optimization consistency objective, and a Native Adaptive Expansion (NAE) decoder that iteratively selects high-confidence variables and enforces constraints via a feasibility projector Γ (Sec. 3.1–3.4; Fig. 1; Eq. 5–8). This design turns diffusion intermediate states into meaningful, feasible partial solutions and yields O(Ts) inference with finite-time convergence under mild assumptions (Prop. 1 in Sec. 3.4). Across TSP, MIS, and CVRP, NEXCO improves solution quality and runtime over strong diffusion and AE baselines—for example, matching or surpassing Fast T2T at 2–4× lower latency on TSP and reducing drops by roughly 50% at similar or lower time (Table 2), with consistent gains on MIS (Table 5) and CVRP (Table 6). The method is robust to imperfect supervision and generalizes across scales and distributions (Sec. 4.1–4.3; Tables 3–4, B.1). Overall, NEXCO unifies feasibility, global awareness, and adaptivity within a single masked diffusion framework for CO.
Q2 (Soundness):
Please assign the paper a numerical rating on the following scale to indicate the soundness of the technical claims, experimental and research methodology and on whether the central claims of the paper are adequately supported with evidence: 4 excellent, 3 good, 2 fair, 1 poor.
The central technical claims are mostly well supported by clear methodology and thorough experiments: - Methodological soundness: The CO-specific one-way mask corruption (Eq. 5, Sec. 3.1), time-agnostic denoiser (Sec. 3.2), TOC objective (Eq. 6, Sec. 3.3), and NAE decoding with feasibility projection (Algorithm 1, Eq. 8, Sec. 3.4) are coherent and well-motivated. The finite-time convergence statement (Proposition 1, Sec. 3.4) is reasonable under stated assumptions (A1–A3). - Empirical support: Consistent quality/runtime gains over strong baselines across TSP, MIS, and CVRP (Table 2, Table 5, Table 6), plus large-scale TSP-10K (Table 8). Robustness and generalization are substantiated by ablations and transfers (Table 3, Table 4, Table 10, Table 11, Table 12; Fig. 3–4), and runtime attribution is provided (Table 13). However, there are a few issues that prevent an “excellent” score: 1) Time-agnostic claim vs. appendix details: Appendix C.1–C.2 include timestep features t and terms involving t0, seemingly contradicting the time-agnostic design asserted in Sec. 3.2. Clarification is needed on whether t is used in the released model. 2) Consistency assumption in Sec. 3.3: The statement “each corrupted state xt is thus a valid subset of x∗” relies on masking the optimal solution; with heuristic labels (Practical note, Sec. 3.3), xt need not be a subset of x∗, weakening the theoretical justification, even if Table 3 shows robustness empirically. 3) Feasibility projection complexity: For TSP, maintaining “subtour-free constraints” (Sec. 3.4) is described as a lightweight boolean predicate, but subtour handling can be nontrivial; more detail on the exact check would strengthen the soundness claim. 4) Minor presentation inconsistencies (e.g., duplicated labels in Table 10) slightly reduce confidence in exact reproducibility. Overall, the methodology is solid and the empirical evidence is strong, with a few inconsistencies that merit clarification. Rating: 3

1. Clear conceptual advance: makes Adaptive Expansion intrinsic to the generative process rather than a wrapper (Table 1; Sec. 1). This resolves both dependence on a GP backbone and the O(Ds·Ts) cost of prior AE (COExpander), yielding O(Ts) complexity while retaining feasibility and global awareness. 2. CO-specific one-way mask corruption (Eq. 5; Sec. 3.1) that unifies [MASK] and 0 into a single background state. This directly addresses the severe 0/1 imbalance and “conservative 0-prediction” failure mode of three-state MDMs on MIS, and turns intermediate states into valid partial solutions. The empirical divergence shown in Fig. 2 convincingly supports this design choice. 3. Time-agnostic denoiser with time-agnostic optimization consistency (Sec. 3.2–3.3; Eq. 6). Removing timestep conditioning exploits the fact that the mask pattern encodes corruption level and eliminates schedule sensitivity. The TOC loss enforces instance-level optimality consistency across corruption levels, aligning training with the optimization objective. 4. Native Adaptive Expansion (NAE) decoding with feasibility projection (Algorithm 1; Eq. 8; Sec. 3.4). The confidence-thresholded candidate selection plus a lightweight, task-specific feasibility predicate produces a monotone trajectory of feasible partial solutions. The abstraction of Γ(·) as a uniform three-stage template clarifies portability across TSP/MIS/CVRP. 5. Formal convergence guarantee under mild assumptions (A1–A3) with a finite-time bound (Proposition 1; Sec. 3.4). This provides a rare bit of theoretical reassurance for a constructive neural decoder and cleanly relates decoding steps to problem structure (e.g., Nmax edges/nodes). 6. Strong and broad empirical gains across diverse CO families with rigorous baselines. Extensive and targeted ablations that isolate where improvements come from: - Corruption scheme: mask vs uniform (Table 10) shows systematic gap reductions. - Decoder mechanism: global non-adaptive schedule vs NAE (Table 11) and greedy full reconstruction vs feasibility projection (Table 12), attributing quality gains to native, confidence-aware expansion rather than extra compute. - Hyperparameters: step budget vs runtime/quality (Fig. 3) and confidence threshold α (Fig. 4), exposing an interpretable efficiency–quality trade-off and robust α ranges. 8. Generalization and scalability evidence beyond in-distribution metrics: - Cross-scale transfer on TSP with notably small gaps (e.g., training on 500 → testing on 1000: 0.37% with NEXCO; Table 4). - Cross-distribution and cross-scale MIS transfer improvements over diffusion baselines (Table 7). - Large-scale TSP-10K with competitive 1.53% gap in 52s (Table 8) and strong TSPLIB performance from a TSP-100-trained model (Table 9). 9. Practical robustness to imperfect supervision (Sec. 4.1; Table 3). Transparent efficiency accounting: runtime breakdown across denoiser and projection costs (Table 13) shows Γ(·) overhead remains small and scales predictably, strengthening the practicality and reproducibility of the claimed O(Ts) behavior. 11. Methodological synthesis grounded in recent discrete/masked diffusion progress (Sec. 2.3) but adapted to CO’s structural constraints. The paper convincingly argues why standard bit-flip diffusion fails to yield meaningful partial states for constructive decoding in CO and demonstrates how MDM-style ideas can be reshaped to respect feasibility (Sec. 3.1–3.4). 12. Generally strong exposition of the pipeline with concrete equations and algorithmic detail (Fig. 1; Eq. 5–8; Algorithm 1) and comprehensive experimental setup and implementation notes (Sec. 4; App. C–D), aiding reproducibility.
Q6 (Weaknesses):
Please give a substantive assessment of the weaknesses of the paper. Focus on constructive and actionable insights on how the work could improve towards its stated goals. Be specific, avoid generic remarks. For example, if you believe the contribution lacks novelty, provide references and an explanation as evidence; if you believe experiments are insufficient, explain why and exactly what is missing, etc. Unclarities are adressed later, so only list real weaknesses. If you have many, only list the least generic ones.
1. Time-agnostic claim is contradicted by the architecture in the appendix. - Evidence: Section 3.2 emphasizes “remove timestep embeddings” and a “time-agnostic denoiser fθ(xt, G)”. Yet Appendix C.1–C.2 include timestep features t and terms “ReLU(t0)” in Eqs. (20)–(21), which suggests denoiser layers use timestep information. - Why it matters: This weakens the central claim that schedule dependence is removed and makes it unclear which variant produced the results. - Actionable: Align the released model with Section 3.2 by removing all timestep conditioning in Appendix C or provide ablations comparing time-agnostic vs time-conditioned denoisers on TSP/MIS/CVRP to quantify the benefit. Explicitly state which variant is used in Tables 2, 5, 6. 2. The “optimization consistency” objective does not enforce cross-level consistency and relies on a strong subset assumption. - Evidence: Time-agnostic optimization consistency in Eq. (6) is just two terms d(fθ(xt′), x*) + d(fθ(xt), x*); there is no term encouraging fθ(xt) ≈ fθ(xt′). Section 3.3 argues “each corrupted state xt is thus a valid subset of x*,” which holds only if the forward process masks the same x* used as the target; the “Practical note” then allows heuristic labels, where the subset claim can be false. - Why it matters: Theoretical justification is weakened, and the objective can degenerate to standard supervised prediction without consistency across masks. - Actionable: Add a cross-level regularizer (e.g., KL or MSE between fθ(xt) and fθ(xt′) restricted to unmasked entries) and report an ablation. Clarify training pairs (is x0 ≡ x*?) and quantify performance when x* is heuristic (beyond Table 3) on MIS/CVRP too. 3. Feasibility projection Γ(·) for TSP underspecified; “subtour-free constraints” are nontrivial. - Evidence: Section 3.4 describes a “lightweight boolean feasibility predicate” that “maintain[s] degree-2 and subtour-free constraints,” but no algorithmic detail is given (e.g., union-find to prevent premature cycles; handling of connectivity and final cycle closure). - Why it matters: Subtour handling can be the practical bottleneck and affects both correctness and time. The claim that Γ is a simple, uniform template hinges on this detail. - Actionable: Specify the exact subtour check (data structures, complexity), and report (i) acceptance ratios per step, (ii) fraction of intermediate states remaining subtour-free, and (iii) time breakdown of subtour tests on TSP in Table 13-style profiling. 4. Convergence guarantee is weak and hinges on a strong expandability assumption that may fail in practice. - Evidence: Proposition 1 in Section 3.4 requires (A2) “Strict expandability” for any incomplete state; this is often violated in constructive TSP/MIS when local commitments create dead-ends. - Why it matters: The result becomes almost tautological and does not inform failure modes or approximation. - Actionable: Empirically report the frequency of getting stuck (no feasible additions) before completion and the recovery strategy (e.g., backtracking/resampling). If possible, provide conditions on Γ(·) (e.g., matroid/greedoid-like structures) under which expandability holds, or incorporate a certified backtrack step into NAE. 5. Fairness gaps in CVRP comparisons; missing strong GP/diffusion baselines. - Evidence: Section 4.3/Table 6 compare only to Sym-NCO (LC) and COExpander (AE), noting “GP-based diffusion solvers have not been extended to CVRP.” - Why it matters: Without a GP/diffusion baseline adapted with a capacity-aware Γ, advantages may reflect task coverage rather than the native AE design. - Actionable: Implement a minimal Fast T2T + feasibility projection on a sparsified graph for CVRP-50/100/200, or report a negative result explaining why it fails (with metrics). Alternatively, include a competitive learned improvement heuristic (e.g., learning k-Opt for CVRP) as an additional baseline. 6. Limited evaluation on real-world routing benchmarks; synthetic-only CVRP distributions. - Evidence: CVRP uses uniform coordinates and demands (Section 4.3) and lacks CVRPLIB or variants with time windows/capacity heterogeneity; by contrast TSPLIB is included for TSP (Table 9). - Why it matters: The paper argues NAE handles hard constraints natively; testing on real-world CVRPLIB and/or VRPTW would better validate Γ’s generality. - Actionable: Add CVRPLIB results under the same setup and one additional constraint variant (e.g., VRPTW small instances) to demonstrate portability of Γ(·). 7. Efficiency reporting could better isolate method-intrinsic gains from hardware and implementation. - Evidence: Section D.1–D.2 mention GPU hardware, yet Section D.1 says “single-threaded setting” for evaluation; Table 13 reports wall-clock times but no FLOPs/NFEs/throughput. Baselines (e.g., Concorde/HGS) are C/C++ heuristics. - Why it matters: Wall-clock times can be sensitive to framework and device. The O(Ts) vs O(Ds·Ts) claim (Table 1; Section 3.4) would be stronger with hardware-neutral metrics. - Actionable: Report denoiser calls (NFEs), average candidates checked, and FLOPs per step for NEXCO vs baselines. Provide GPU vs CPU timings to show robustness of the speedups. 8. Data/label efficiency not addressed despite heavy supervision. - Evidence: Training uses up to 1.28M labeled instances for TSP-100 (Section 4.1) with Concorde supervision; similar scale for CVRP with HGS (Section 4.3). Only one label robustness ablation is shown for TSP (Table 3). - Why it matters: Label generation is expensive; data efficiency is important for practical adoption. - Actionable: Provide (i) training compute/label generation time, (ii) performance vs training set size curves, and (iii) results with weak/partial labels or self-training on MIS/CVRP (not just TSP). 9. “Adaptive” expansion still relies on fixed hyperparameters α and ρt schedules. - Evidence: Algorithm 1/Section 3.4 and Section 4.4 select a fixed threshold α and a pre-set ρt schedule; Figure 4 shows sensitivity. - Why it matters: Fixed schedules may undercut the “instance-adaptive” decoding claim. - Actionable: Learn α and ρt from the denoiser’s uncertainty (e.g., entropy/quantile thresholds) or via a small policy network; report gains and robustness across scales. 10. Inconsistencies around step notation and complexity may confuse the native-vs-wrapper advantage. - Evidence: Table 1 states NEXCO is O(Ts), while experiments vary D s (Table 2, Table 6), and Algorithm 1 uses “iterations Ts, threshold α, expansion schedule {ρt}”. This muddles whether Ts or Ds controls expansion. - Why it matters: The core claim is complexity O(Ts) without the wrapper’s O(Ds·Ts). - Actionable: Unify notation (use one symbol) and explicitly map “expansion rounds” to the complexity variable in Table 1. Add a plot of gap vs number of denoiser calls for NEXCO, Fast T2T, and COExpander to visualize the O(Ts) benefit. 11. MIS coverage omits some baselines/scales; evidence could be strengthened. - Evidence: Table 5 lacks entries for several GP baselines on RB-[800–1200] and ER; DIMES entries are partial. Figure 2 shows validation trends but no quantitative ablation across p for the corruption design. - Why it matters: The case for CO-specific mask vs three-state MDM on MIS is strong qualitatively; fuller quantitative comparisons would solidify it. - Actionable: Fill missing MIS baseline rows where feasible, or explain failures/timeouts. Add a controlled MIS study across ER p ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4} comparing three-state MDM vs CO-specific corruption with size/drop/time. 12. Generality beyond binary decisions is not discussed. - Evidence: The framework is built around binary x ∈ {0,1}^N (Section 2.1), with one-way 1→0 corruption (Eq. 5). - Why it matters: Many CO problems require multi-way categorical choices (e.g., assignment, coloring). - Actionable: Outline how NEXCO would extend to k-ary variables (e.g., mask-drop of active category, categorical Γ(·)), and include a small-scale k-ary benchmark or a proof-of-concept ablation.
