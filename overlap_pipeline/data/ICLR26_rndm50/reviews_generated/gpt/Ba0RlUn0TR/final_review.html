Q1 (Summary):
Briefly summarize the paper and its contributions. Do not critique the paper here. The authors should agree with a well-written summary. Typical range: 3-8 sentences.
The paper introduces Partition-Losses Fine-Tuning (PL), a post-training defense for backdoored models that leverages two small tuning pools: a mostly-clean benign pool and a quarantined pool of flagged (mostly triggered) samples with a known target label. Instead of indirectly suppressing the backdoor via clean-only fine-tuning, PL directly unlearns the trigger-to-target association by minimizing cross-entropy on benign data while maximizing the target-class cross-entropy on flagged inputs, with a trade-off weight α (Section 3; Algorithm 1). The authors provide a surrogate-theoretic guarantee showing that, under imperfect pools, PL’s gradient steps reduce attack success more than clean-only fine-tuning when the malicious pool is not overly contaminated (Theorem 1; Appendix A). Extensive experiments on CIFAR-10, GTSRB, and Tiny-ImageNet across five attacks (BadNet, Blended, SSBA, WaNet, Adaptive Blend) show that PL matches or surpasses state-of-the-art clean-only fine-tuning while using 2–2.5× fewer clean samples (Table 1), and remains effective under realistic contamination in either or both pools (Tables 2–3). Sensitivity studies indicate PL is stable across α choices and tuning data budgets (Figures 2–3). The method is plug-and-play, architecture- and domain-agnostic, and adds only a minor per-iteration overhead (Section 3).
Q2 (Soundness):
Please assign the paper a numerical rating on the following scale to indicate the soundness of the technical claims, experimental and research methodology and on whether the central claims of the paper are adequately supported with evidence: 4 excellent, 3 good, 2 fair, 1 poor.
The core claims are well supported by both analysis and extensive experiments. Theorem 1 (Section 3; Appendix A) gives a clear surrogate guarantee under explicit contamination assumptions (ϵm < 1/2, ∥gclean∥ ≤ ∥gtrig∥), and the optimization procedure is simple and sound (Algorithm 1). Empirically, PL consistently attains the lowest ASR with competitive C-ACC under ideal partitioning while using fewer clean samples (Table 1a–b), and maintains strong performance under contamination in the benign pool, malicious pool, and both pools (Tables 2–3). Sensitivity to α and data budget shows stable behavior without per-attack tuning (Figures 2–3). The main theoretical limitation is the reliance on a surrogate ASR and nontrivial assumptions, but the comprehensive empirical validation across datasets, architectures, and five attacks mitigates this concern. Rating: 3

Q5 (Strengths):
Please give a substantive assessment of the strengths of the paper. Be specific, avoid generic remarks. This can, for example, include quality, clarity (e.g. well written), or significance, originality (e.g. a new definition or problem formulation, creative combinations of existing ideas, application to a new domain, or removing limitations from prior results.) If you have many, only list the least generic ones.
1. Realistic and novel fine-tuning setting. Section 3 formalizes two tuning pools with explicit contamination rates ϵb and ϵm, reflecting flagged “mostly malicious” samples with a known target and a “mostly clean” pool. This removes a central limitation of clean-only fine-tuning that assumes a perfectly clean set (Section 1; “Both pools may be imperfect” in Problem settings). 2. Simple, plug-in objective that directly unlearns the backdoor. The PL loss explicitly minimizes CE on D b and maximizes target-class CE on D m (Eq. (1); Algorithm 1), requiring “only one extra forward pass per iteration” and “no architectural changes” (Section 3). This is a creative but minimal modification with immediate practical appeal. 3. Clear theoretical justification under imperfect partitions. Theorem 1 (Section 3; Appendix A) provides (i) a relative improvement guarantee vs. clean-only FT and (ii) a sufficient condition for absolute decrease of surrogate ASR, under explicit, interpretable conditions (ϵm < 1/2 and ∥gclean∥ ≤ ∥gtrig∥). The gradient-level analysis matches the design of the loss. 4. Strong data efficiency with explicit accounting of tuning budgets. Under ideal partitioning, PL achieves the lowest ASR while using 2–2.5× fewer clean samples than baselines (Table 1a–b). For PL-5, grand mean ASR is 0.004 with C-ACC 0.784, versus FT 0.712 ASR and FT+SAM 0.759 ASR (Table 1a). On Tiny-ImageNet, PL attains mean C-ACC 0.693 with ASR 0.000, while I-BAU and FST reach ASR 0.000 but collapse utility (I-BAU C-ACC 0.005; FST 0.401; Table 1a). 5. Robustness to realistic contamination in either/both pools. With ϵb=0.1 (benign contamination), PL retains the best safety–utility trade-off (grand mean ASR 0.107, C-ACC 0.760), while FT+SAM keeps utility but leaves high ASR (0.787) (Table 2a). With ϵm=0.1 (malicious contamination), PL maintains near-zero ASR (0.002) at competitive C-ACC (0.656), substantially outperforming FT+SAM and FT on ASR (Table 2b). With both pools contaminated (ϵb=ϵm=0.1), PL still attains near-zero ASR (0.010) with reasonable utility (0.661), whereas FT/FT+SAM/FST keep higher C-ACC but have large ASR (Table 2c). Similar patterns hold at larger budgets (Table 3). 6. Stability to hyperparameters and data volume. Figure 2 shows monotonic ASR reduction as α increases with modest C-ACC decay, supporting a single default α=0.2 without per-attack tuning. Figure 3 demonstrates consistent lowest ASR across 2/5/10% tuning fractions under both perfect and contaminated partitions, highlighting robustness over data availability. 7. Breadth and difficulty of empirical evaluation. Experiments span three standard datasets with different architectures (ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10/GTSRB; Swin Transformer on Tiny-ImageNet; Section 4), and five diverse attacks including SSBA’s invisible, sample-specific triggers and Adaptive Blend’s spatially sparse patterns (Section 4; Appendix B). PL’s dominance is not limited to easy attacks/datasets. 8. Cross-attack generalization. Appendix E (Tables 4–5) shows that PL trained on one attack retains low ASR and high C-ACC when evaluated on unseen attacks, and degrades gracefully under contamination—valuable for incident-response settings where the exact trigger may be uncertain. 9. Well-scoped research questions and transparent accounting. The work states concrete questions (Q1–Q3; Section 4), quantifies tuning budgets and effective clean data (Section 4.1), and contrasts “grand means” across methods (Tables 1–3), improving interpretability of gains beyond isolated numbers. 10. Practicality for real pipelines. The paper explicitly connects to inference-stage detectors that can flag suspicious inputs/target classes (Section 1), and the proposed method is “architecture- and domain-agnostic” with minimal compute overhead (Section 3), making deployment feasible without retraining from scratch. 11. Reproducibility support. The method is fully specified (Eq. (1); Algorithm 1), datasets/architectures/attack details and defense configurations are documented (Appendix B), and sensitivity/ablation plots (Figures 2–3; Appendix D/E) provide actionable guidance for practitioners.
Q6 (Weaknesses):
Please give a substantive assessment of the weaknesses of the paper. Focus on constructive and actionable insights on how the work could improve towards its stated goals. Be specific, avoid generic remarks. For example, if you believe the contribution lacks novelty, provide references and an explanation as evidence; if you believe experiments are insufficient, explain why and exactly what is missing, etc. Unclarities are adressed later, so only list real weaknesses. If you have many, only list the least generic ones.
1. Reliance on a known target class and flagged triggers, without testing mis-specification. - Evidence: Problem settings in Section 3 assume a malicious pool Dm “with known target t,” and all experiments fix “t = 0” (Attack Settings, Section 4). Theorem 1 also optimizes CE(·, t). - Weakness: Real pipelines can misidentify the target or face multi-target/backdoors. - Actionable: Add robustness tests where (i) the provided target is wrong (top-1 wrong and top-k includes the true target), (ii) there are multiple target classes, and (iii) the target shifts over time. Report ASR/C-ACC and recovery strategies (e.g., optimizing against several candidate targets). 2. Narrow attack coverage: only dirty-label attacks; no clean-label or label-consistent backdoors. - Evidence: “five representative dirty-label backdoor attacks” (Section 4). - Weakness: Many realistic threats are clean-label/label-consistent (e.g., Turner et al., 2019, cited), source-specific, or multi-target; PL’s objective that maximizes loss on t may behave differently. - Actionable: Evaluate on clean-label/label-consistent (Turner et al., 2019) and source-label-specific/multi-target variants to substantiate generality. Include at least one adaptive variant where the attacker anticipates target-class suppression. 3. Computational budget fairness and overhead are not quantified. - Evidence: PL “adds one additional forward pass per iteration” (Section 3; Algorithm 1 samples both Db and Dm each step). Claims “same computational budget” (Abstract) but Section 4.1 compares different data fractions (e.g., PL-5 vs. baselines 10%). - Weakness: Per-iteration cost is higher for PL; comparisons should control for compute, not only sample counts. - Actionable: Report wall-clock time and approximate FLOPs per method. Provide head-to-head results under equalized compute (e.g., same total forward/backward passes) and under equal total samples seen. This will strengthen the efficiency claim. 4. Baseline comparison confound due to unequal tuning budgets. - Evidence: Section 4.1: baselines use 10% (or 5%) vs. PL’s 5% (or 2%), and “effective clean” sample counts differ. - Weakness: While favorable to PL (wins with less clean data), it leaves open whether baselines close the ASR gap under equal total budget or if PL still dominates when everyone gets the same total (clean + flagged) count. - Actionable: Add a control where all methods use the same total tuning set size and steps; also let baselines see the same number of clean samples as PL to isolate the effect of leveraging flagged data. 5. No statistical robustness reporting. - Evidence: Tables 1–3 report single-point estimates; Appendix B specifies epochs but not seeds/runs. - Weakness: Without multiple seeds/variances, it is unclear if differences are statistically significant, especially where C-ACC gaps are modest (e.g., Table 1a Tiny-ImageNet). - Actionable: Run ≥3 seeds and report mean ± std for ASR and C-ACC; include significance testing or confidence intervals. This is especially important for the “grand mean” summaries. 6. Theoretical assumptions are not validated empirically. - Evidence: Theorem 1 requires ϵm < 1/2 and ∥gclean∥ ≤ ∥gtrig∥; it analyzes a surrogate ASR. - Weakness: The paper does not measure whether these assumptions hold in practice or how surrogate changes track true ASR reductions. - Actionable: (i) Empirically estimate ∥gtrig∥ and ∥gclean∥ during tuning to check the assumption, (ii) plot step-wise changes in surrogate vs. true ASR, and (iii) stress-test near the ϵm ≈ 0.5 boundary to map failure modes and the α needed by the sufficient condition in Theorem 1. 7. Contamination modeling is abstract; no detector-sourced partitions are used. - Evidence: The paper simulates contamination via fixed ϵb, ϵm (Section 3; Section 4.2; Appendix D) and motivates with inference-stage detectors (Section 1). - Weakness: Fixed-rate contamination may not reflect the correlated errors and target hypotheses produced by practical detectors. - Actionable: Construct Db/Dm using a concrete detector (e.g., Neural Cleanse/TABOR, as cited in Section 1) to create flagged samples and an inferred target; evaluate PL end-to-end under that pipeline and its ROC trade-offs. 8. Single target choice and fixed poison rate limit external validity. - Evidence: “The target label is set to t = 0 for all attacks” and “poison rate ρ = 0.15” (Section 4.1). - Weakness: Results may vary with different targets (class difficulty/imbalance) and poisoning strengths. - Actionable: Vary t across several classes per dataset and evaluate multiple poison rates (e.g., 5%, 10%, 20%). Report how α or data budget should be adjusted under these regimes. 9. Cross-attack generalization is shown primarily on CIFAR-10. - Evidence: Appendix E (Tables 4–5) present cross-attack matrices that appear to be on CIFAR-10. - Weakness: It remains unclear if the generalization holds for GTSRB and Tiny-ImageNet, where architectures/difficulties differ. - Actionable: Extend cross-attack evaluation to GTSRB and Tiny-ImageNet to support the claim of architecture- and domain-agnostic behavior. 10. Limited analysis of side effects on non-target classes and calibration. - Evidence: PL maximizes CE on the target t for flagged inputs (Eq. (1)); contamination implies some clean samples of other labels in Dm (ϵm > 0). - Weakness: Suppressing the target logit could inadvertently depress target-class performance more broadly or alter class-wise calibration. - Actionable: Report per-class accuracy and confusion matrices before/after PL, especially for the target class; optionally include calibration metrics (e.g., ECE) to ensure PL does not degrade reliability disproportionately.
Q7 (Suggestions)
Please list and carefully describe any questions and suggestions for the authors. Think of the things where a response from the author can change your opinion, clarify a confusion or address a limitation. This is important for a productive rebuttal and discussion phase with the authors. Do not simply repeat all the weaknesses.
1. Assumption of known target t and mis-specification - Section 3 assumes Dm comes “with known target t,” and all experiments fix t = 0 (Section 4, Attack Settings). How robust is PL if the flagged target is wrong or ambiguous? Please add ablations where (i) t is mis-specified (top-1 wrong while the true target is in top-k), (ii) several candidate targets are provided and PL maximizes over them, and (iii) multi-target backdoors are present. Report ASR/C-ACC and any selection strategy that mitigates mis-specification. 2. End-to-end construction of Dm via real detectors - The Introduction cites inference-stage detectors (Neural Cleanse, TABOR, etc.). Could you instantiate Db/Dm and the inferred target using one such detector, then run PL end-to-end? Vary the detector operating point (ROC) to induce correlated, non-i.i.d. contamination and report PL’s ASR/C-ACC vs detector FP/FN (Section 4.2 framing). This would directly validate the intended deployment. 3. Theoretical assumptions: empirical validation and boundary behavior - Theorem 1 relies on ϵm < 1/2 and ∥gclean∥ ≤ ∥gtrig∥. Please (i) estimate these gradient norms during tuning to check the assumption, (ii) plot step-wise changes of the surrogate ˜ASR alongside true ASR to verify the surrogate’s faithfulness, and (iii) stress-test near ϵm ≈ 0.5 to map failure modes and whether larger α satisfies the sufficient condition in Theorem 1 (Section 3; Appendix A). 4. Compute and budget fairness - The Abstract claims “same computational budget” while Section 3 adds “one additional forward pass” and Section 4.1 varies the data fractions (PL-5 vs baselines 10%; PL-2 vs baselines 5%). Please provide head-to-head comparisons under: (a) equalized compute (same total forward/backward passes or FLOPs; include wall-clock), and (b) equal total tuning set size. Do PL’s ASR gains persist under these controls? 5. What if baselines could also use Dm (without the target label)? - To isolate the value of flagged data vs the PL loss, let clean-only baselines train on Db ∪ Dm but ignore t (e.g., standard CE on labeled clean samples only). Does simply mixing flagged images (treated as unlabeled or excluded from loss) help, and how does that compare to PL? 6. Statistical robustness and variance - Tables 1–3 report single numbers. Please add results over multiple runs (≥3 seeds) with mean ± std for ASR and C-ACC, and indicate statistical significance for the “grand mean” comparisons. This is important where C-ACC gaps are modest (e.g., Tiny-ImageNet in Table 1a). 7. Per-class effects and calibration - Since PL suppresses p(t|·) on Dm, could it harm the legitimate target class? Please report per-class accuracy and confusion matrices before/after PL, focusing on class t, and include calibration (e.g., ECE) to ensure the procedure does not degrade reliability or disproportionately penalize t on clean data (Section 4.2 contaminated Dm implies some clean y = t may appear). 8. Diversity of attacks beyond dirty-label - All five evaluated attacks are dirty-label (Section 4). Could you include at least one clean-label or label-consistent attack (e.g., Turner et al., 2019, cited in Section 2), and a multi-target/source-specific variant? This would strengthen generality claims for Eq. (1) when the true label is consistent with the target. 9. Multiple triggers and targets - SSBA covers sample-specific triggers, but what about multiple coexisting triggers and/or multiple target classes within the same model? Please test PL when Dm aggregates examples from different triggers and possibly different targets (with either a single or multiple t’s provided), and describe how PL should be scheduled (e.g., interleave targets or maximize a sum over targets). 10. Sensitivity to poison rate and target identity - Section 4 uses a fixed poison rate ρ = 0.15 and t = 0 for all attacks. Please vary ρ (e.g., 5%, 10%, 20%) and sweep t across several classes per dataset to assess whether PL’s α or data budget needs adjustment, and whether results depend on class difficulty/imbalance. 11. Adaptive α selection - Figures 2–3 show stable behavior for α ∈ {0.1,…,0.5} with a default α = 0.2. Can you propose and test a simple rule to adapt α on-the-fly (e.g., guided by the ratio of empirical gradients on Dm vs Db suggested by Theorem 1’s sufficient condition)? A data-driven α could further improve robustness under shifting ϵb/ϵm. 12. Cross-attack generalization beyond CIFAR-10 - Appendix E (Tables 4–5) demonstrates cross-attack generalization that appears limited to CIFAR-10. Please extend the cross-attack matrices to GTSRB and Tiny-ImageNet to support the claim that PL is architecture- and domain-agnostic (Section 3; Section 4). 13. Interaction with the target logit on clean Dm contamination - Under malicious-pool contamination (ϵm > 0 in Section 4.2, Tables 2–3), Dm includes clean samples with various ground-truth labels, including y = t. How often does PL push down the correct target logit for such clean y = t samples, and what is the net effect on the true target-class performance? A stratified analysis of Dm’s clean portion by y would clarify this trade-off. 14. Alternative objectives and ablations - Why choose maximizing CE(·, t) rather than, e.g., maximizing margin against t or encouraging uniform distributions on Dm? A brief ablation (same compute) comparing CE-max vs a margin-based variant or uniform-KL on triggered samples would help justify Eq. (1) as the best trade-off. 15. Representation drift analysis - On Tiny-ImageNet, some baselines show drastic utility drops while PL maintains higher C-ACC with ASR ≈ 0 (Table 1a). Could you include a representation drift analysis (e.g., cosine similarity between penultimate-layer features or classifier weights pre-/post-tuning) to illustrate how PL avoids deleterious drift relative to FST/FT+SAM? 16. Reporting and clarity fixes - Several tables and Algorithm 1 appear garbled in the provided text (duplicate headers, broken columns, mixed rows; e.g., Tables 1–3; Algorithm 1). For the camera-ready, please ensure all tables/figures are cleanly rendered, units and splits (per-pool vs total) are unambiguous, and attack names are consistent (e.g., “Blended,” not “Blend/visible”). Including exact N, Ntune, per-pool sizes, and training-time details in Section 4/App. B would aid reproducibility. These additions and clarifications would materially strengthen the empirical and practical claims, and help verify that the key assumptions behind Theorem 1 and the PL objective hold in realistic, detector-driven, and potentially mis-specified settings.
