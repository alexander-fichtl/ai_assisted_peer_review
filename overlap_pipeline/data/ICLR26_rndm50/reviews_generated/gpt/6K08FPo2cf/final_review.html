Q1 (Summary):
Briefly summarize the paper and its contributions. Do not critique the paper here. The authors should agree with a well-written summary. Typical range: 3-8 sentences.
This paper introduces Matting Anything 2 (MAM2), a promptable video matting framework that supports points, boxes, or masks and requires user input only on the first frame (Figure 2, Section 3.1). The core technical contribution is the Promptable Dual-mode Decoder (PDD), which jointly predicts a segmentation mask and a high-quality trimap via mask-guided fusion to strengthen trimap supervision and improve generalization (Section 3.4, Figure 3). To stabilize trimap prediction for transparent objects across time, the authors propose the Memory-Separable Siamese (MSS) mechanism: a two-pass recurrent decoding where the second pass uses memory-free features and the first-pass mask as a pseudo-prompt, sharing weights and adding no parameters (Section 3.5, Figure 4). Training employs a selective supervision scheme that first optimizes the main model on VOS/VM/IM for mask/trimap outputs and then trains a lightweight trimap-based matter (MEMatte) only on high-quality image matting data to refine the alpha (Section 3.2, Figure 6). The paper also introduces the Natural Object Video Matting (NOVM) benchmark with broad category diversity for evaluation (Section 4.2, Table 9). Experiments show state-of-the-art results on NOVM and YoutubeMatte in interactive and automatic modes, and strong image matting performance on AIM500 (Table 2, Table 3, Table 4), with ablations confirming the impact of PDD and MSS (Table 5).

Reasoning: The technical claims are clearly specified and implemented (Sections 3.4–3.5). The methodology is sound: a progressive pipeline, selective supervision (Section 3.2, Figure 6), explicit loss definitions (Appendix A.5), and sufficient training details (Section 4.1). Evidence is strong: large gains on NOVM and YoutubeMatte in interactive mode (Table 2), in automatic mode (Table 3), and on image matting (Table 4). Ablations isolate the effects of PDD and MSS and validate the “memory-free second pass” hypothesis (Table 5), and a training-set control shows improvements persist when aligning datasets (Table 8). Qualitative figures support temporal stability for transparent objects (Figure 4) and improved trimaps (Figure 3). Limitations are acknowledged (Section E). Remaining caveats include that NOVM is relatively small and composited (Section 4.2, Table 9), some prompt/evaluation protocol details are light, and efficiency is lower (Table 10). Overall, the central claims are well supported. Rating: 3

Q4 (Contribution):
Please assign the paper a numerical rating on the following scale to indicate the quality of the overall contribution this paper makes to the research area being studied. Are the questions being asked important? Does the paper bring a significant originality of ideas and/or execution? Are the results valuable to share with the broader NeurIPS community? 4 excellent, 3 good, 2 fair, 1 poor.
The paper tackles an important gap—promptable video matting for diverse, including transparent, objects—beyond portrait-centric settings. It introduces two simple yet original components with clear practical impact: the Promptable Dual-mode Decoder (PDD) for joint mask/trimap prediction with mask-guided fusion (Section 3.4, Figure 3) and the Memory-Separable Siamese mechanism (MSS) to stabilize trimaps by decoupling memory during the second pass (Section 3.5, Figure 4). The Natural Object Video Matting benchmark expands evaluation breadth (Section 4.2, Table 9). Results show large, consistent gains across interactive and automatic video matting and strong image matting performance (Tables 2–4), with focused ablations and controls that isolate the contributions and rule out training-set confounds (Table 5, Table 8). Despite some efficiency trade-offs (Table 10) and the modest, composited nature of NOVM, the technical advances and demonstrated versatility are valuable to the community. Rating: 4
Q5 (Strengths):
Please give a substantive assessment of the strengths of the paper. Be specific, avoid generic remarks. This can, for example, include quality, clarity (e.g. well written), or significance, originality (e.g. a new definition or problem formulation, creative combinations of existing ideas, application to a new domain, or removing limitations from prior results.) If you have many, only list the least generic ones.
1. Clear and consequential problem framing. The paper precisely diagnoses two practical blockers in video matting—domain specificity and “first-frame mask” reliance—and explains why these break on transparent/intricate objects (Section 1; Table 1), motivating promptable, non-mask interactions. 2. Promptable Dual-mode Decoder (PDD) is a concrete, effective architectural idea. It jointly predicts a mask and trimap and explicitly injects the mask into a lightweight fusion path to guide trimap decoding (Section 3.4; Figure 2b). This is not a trivial parallel head: the mask-guided fusion materially stabilizes trimaps (Figure 3) and yields large quantitative gains (top rows of Table 5). 3. Memory-Separable Siamese (MSS) is a well-justified fix to a specific failure mode. The paper identifies that memory-embedded features bias trimaps toward binary masks in later frames and separates trimap decoding from mask memory by a second, memory-free pass driven by the first-pass mask (Section 3.5; Equations 6–7; Figure 2c). The ablation cleanly shows why “just another pass” (MCS) doesn’t help while memory-free features do (bottom rows of Table 5); qualitative evidence on bubbles is compelling (Figure 4). 4. Strong, flexible interaction design. The system supports points, boxes, or masks directly on the first frame with no auxiliary interactive segmenter (Section 3.1; Figure 1), yet still achieves top results when prompted only with points/boxes (Table 2, “Box & Point”). 5. Selective Supervision Scheme is a practical training contribution. The two-stage protocol aligns supervision quality with task difficulty—learning coarse mask/trimap from mixed VOS/VM/IM and refining alpha only from high-fidelity image matting data (Section 3.2; Equations 1–2; Figure 6). This leverages heterogeneous data without overfitting to noisy VM labels. 6. Broad, convincing empirical validation across modes and domains. The method establishes large margins on NOVM and YoutubeMatte in interactive mode (Table 2; e.g., MAD 14.72 on NOVM with boxes/points vs. 39.44 for MatAnyone), outperforms in automatic mode (Table 3), and is competitive/best on image matting without text prompts (Table 4). Multiple metrics (MAD, MSE, Grad, dtSSD) substantiate quality, detail fidelity, and temporal stability. 7. Careful controls and ablations strengthen the causal narrative. In addition to the mechanism-level ablations (Table 5), the training-set control (Table 8) shows the performance edge persists when aligning datasets with FTP-VM, mitigating concerns that gains are due to data composition. 8. Useful new benchmark with diverse categories. NOVM fills a real evaluation gap by covering plants, fire, water, cloud, etc., with balanced categories and clean mattes (Section 4.2; Table 9; Figure 7), enabling assessment “beyond portraits” that prior test sets lacked (Table 1). 9. Practicality in deployment despite a modest efficiency trade-off. MSS shares parameters (no added params) and has negligible overhead (Section 3.5). While MAM2’s base is heavier, total deployed parameters can be lower than systems that require large auxiliary models for first-frame masks (Table 10), simplifying end-to-end use. 10. High reproducibility and transparency. The paper details optimization schedules, LoRA settings, trainable parameter counts, and losses (Section 4.1; Appendix A.5), enumerates training datasets and sampling probabilities (Appendix A.3; Table 6; Table 7), and commits to public data only (Section 4.1), making the work straightforward to replicate. 11. Clear, insight-driven analysis of failure modes. The explanation that mask-memory embedding shifts feature space and biases trimap decoding toward foreground is explicit and tied to evidence (Section 3.5; Figure 4), improving conceptual clarity beyond reporting numerical gains.
Q6 (Weaknesses):
Please give a substantive assessment of the weaknesses of the paper. Focus on constructive and actionable insights on how the work could improve towards its stated goals. Be specific, avoid generic remarks. For example, if you believe the contribution lacks novelty, provide references and an explanation as evidence; if you believe experiments are insufficient, explain why and exactly what is missing, etc. Unclarities are adressed later, so only list real weaknesses. If you have many, only list the least generic ones.
1. NOVM construction and external validity - Evidence: Section 4.2 states NOVM is built by “collect[ing] After Effects assets that included pre-existing alpha mattes” and compositing them onto dynamic backgrounds; it contains only 50 clips (Table 9). - Why it matters: Composited assets may under-represent sensor noise, motion blur, rolling shutter, exposure changes, and complex light transport that drive real temporal failure modes in video matting. With 50 clips, category coverage is broad but statistical power is limited. - Suggestions: - Add a real-captured test subset with human-verified alphas for a few categories (e.g., water, smoke, plants), or include an existing non-portrait set with real footage (e.g., parts of VideoMatting108 include smoke/cloth; Table 1). - Report per-category NOVM scores and opacity-stratified results (e.g., bins over mean alpha) to show robustness on highly transparent regions. - Provide a cross-dataset test on any available real videos beyond portraits (quantitative), not only qualitative results (Figures 11–12). 2. Interaction protocol and user-effort comparability - Evidence: Table 2 compares methods that consume different first-frame inputs (trimap/mask vs MAM2’s “Box & Point”); Section 3.1/ Figure 1 emphasize flexible prompting. - Why it matters: Results with points/boxes depend strongly on click budget, click strategy, and box tightness; without a standardized protocol, the “interaction cost” claim is hard to substantiate and comparisons across input types can be misleading. - Suggestions: - Specify and follow a standard interactive protocol (e.g., fixed click budgets, error-driven clicking, randomized box jitter) and plot quality vs number of clicks for MAM2. - Add sensitivity analyses to box tightness and point noise. Include “box-only” and “point-only” curves. - For fairness, also evaluate baselines in a regime where a first-frame mask is provided to MAM2 (you partly do in Table 2) and report results at matched user-effort levels (e.g., MAM2 with 0/1/3 clicks vs baselines with oracle trimap/mask). 3. Efficiency and resource reporting - Evidence: Table 10 shows MAM2 is slower (11.24 FPS) and heavier (256.83M params) than some baselines; it attributes large “Total Params” to competitors’ auxiliary models (e.g., SAM-Huge for MatAnyone). - Why it matters: Counting auxiliary models depends on deployment choices; conversely, MSS’s two-pass decoding and storing memory-free features add runtime and memory not analyzed. - Suggestions: - Report apples-to-apples latency and memory under standardized scenarios: (a) first-frame mask provided to all methods (no auxiliary models), (b) first-frame interactive segmentation via a common small SAM2 backbone for all. - Break down per-frame latency across components (encoder, first PDD, second PDD, matter) and report peak GPU memory, FLOPs, and resolution scaling. - Quantify the overhead of MSS (second pass) and the memory footprint of caching Fnon-mem on long, high-resolution videos. 4. Modest novelty of PDD relative to prior SAM-based matting - Evidence: Section 3.4 notes SEMatte uses a parallel branch to predict trimaps; PDD adds mask-guided fusion (Figure 2b) to stabilize trimaps (Figure 3). - Why it matters: The conceptual step from a parallel head (SEMatte) to mask-guided fusion is incremental; the paper’s main leap appears to be MSS. - Suggestions: - Strengthen the technical positioning by comparing PDD against richer conditioning mechanisms (e.g., cross-attention from the mask feature, FiLM/affine modulation, or joint consistency losses between mask/trimap). - Provide ablations on these alternatives and a failure/success taxonomy (beyond Figure 3) to motivate why the proposed fusion specifically yields the observed gains. 5. MSS validation and alternatives - Evidence: Section 3.5 attributes temporal collapse to “feature space shift” after memory embedding and fixes it by re-decoding trimap on Fnon-mem with the first-pass mask (Equations 6–7). Table 5 contrasts MSS with “MCS” (second pass on memory-embedded features). - Why it matters: While effective, MSS is a heuristic architectural choice; the underlying hypothesis is not quantitatively tested, and other principled designs (e.g., dual memory banks) are not explored. - Suggestions: - Provide diagnostics quantifying the hypothesized feature shift (e.g., cosine distances between Fmem and Fnon-mem at trimap-relevant locations, class-separability analyses) and the correlation with trimap errors for transparent objects (Figure 4 case). - Evaluate alternative remedies: separate memory for trimap decoding, a light adapter to “unwind” memory attention before trimap head, or joint training with a penalty that discourages mask-induced foreground bias in the trimap. - Analyze temporal stability as a function of sequence length to show that MSS’s consistency indeed propagates over time (beyond dtSSD aggregates). 6. Training scheme and trimap supervision quality - Evidence: Section 3.2 and Appendix A.2 supervise trimaps by degrading image-matting alphas via dilation/erosion/quantization during Stage 1, then train the matter only on images in Stage 2. - Why it matters: Morphological trimap synthesis tends to create narrow, uniform “unknown” bands around opaque objects and may not reflect large, spatially varying unknown regions typical of transparent phenomena; Stage 2 lacks video supervision and could bias toward static detail priors. - Suggestions: - Compare different trimap synthesis strategies (variable-width unknown regions, opacity-aware unknown sampling) and include a small set of manually curated trimaps for transparent objects to improve Stage 1 realism. - Add an ablation of the two-stage scheme vs. joint end-to-end training and vs. training Stage 2 with a small amount of video matting data (even if lower-fidelity) to assess temporal generalization of the matter. - Report how Stage 2 affects temporal metrics (dtSSD) to ensure the image-only refinement does not harm stability. 7. Benchmarking breadth on non-portrait videos - Evidence: Non-portrait results are reported only on NOVM (Table 2); automatic mode is evaluated only on human datasets (Table 3). - Why it matters: Claims of “matting anything” would be stronger with corroboration on additional non-portrait video benchmarks or automatic settings. - Suggestions: - Add quantitative results on VideoMatting108 (Table 1 lists it includes “cloth, smoke”) or any other public non-portrait videos, even if limited, and discuss domain gaps. - Provide an “automatic” evaluation on NOVM using a generic objectness/box detector to produce the first-frame prompt, mirroring the YouTubeMatte automatic setting (Section 4.3). 8. Backend generality claim untested - Evidence: Section 3.1 says “MAM2 is compatible with trimap-based matte model of various architectures,” yet experiments fix MEMatte (Appendix A.1). - Why it matters: If compatibility is a key claim, showing performance across at least one alternative matter (e.g., ViTMatte or another trimap-based model) would substantiate generality. - Suggestions: - Swap MEMatte for a second lightweight matting head and report NOVM/YouTubeMatte results, or present a small study comparing a heavier vs lighter matter to illustrate the quality–efficiency trade-off and portability. 9. Limited analysis of segmentation quality - Evidence: The pipeline predicts both M and T (Section 3.4), but reported metrics focus on alpha quality (Tables 2–3). - Why it matters: Since PDD changes mask/trimap decoding jointly, it is important to ensure mask quality is not degraded (affecting editors who also use masks). - Suggestions: - Report standard segmentation metrics (e.g., IoU/F-score) for the predicted mask M on the first frame and across time (with any available mask GT, e.g., VOS data used in training), or at least on YouTubeMatte where portrait masks can be derived from alpha.

1. NOVM construction and validity - Section 4.2 states NOVM uses After Effects assets composited on dynamic backgrounds. Please detail the sourcing and licensing of assets, compositing pipeline (blending, motion blur, exposure changes), and whether any sensor noise/rolling-shutter was synthesized. Could you release scripts to reproduce the compositing? - Provide per-category scores and opacity-stratified results (e.g., bins by mean alpha) on NOVM (Table 9). This would clarify robustness on highly transparent phenomena. - Add a small real-captured non-portrait subset (with human-verified alphas) or a cross-dataset quantitative test beyond portraits (e.g., the smoke/cloth in VideoMatting108 from Table 1). This would strengthen external validity. 2. Interactive prompting protocol - For the “Box & Point” setting in Table 2, please specify the interaction budget and strategy: number of clicks, click placement policy (error-driven vs. random), box tightness/jitter, and whether mid-sequence corrections are allowed (SAM2 supports this; Section 3.1). - Report quality vs interaction curves (0/1/3/5 clicks) and sensitivity to box tightness and point noise. For fairness, also show MAM2 with an oracle first-frame mask (already partially in Table 2) at matched user effort. 3. MSS overhead and scaling - Section 3.5 notes the second PDD pass has “negligible” overhead. Please quantify per-frame latency and peak memory for each component (encoder, first/second PDD, matter) and show how overhead scales with resolution and sequence length. Including FLOPs and peak GPU memory would help interpret Table 10. 4. MSS mechanism diagnostics - The hypothesis is that memory attention shifts features so trimap decoding collapses to masks (Section 3.5, Figure 4). Can you quantify feature shift (e.g., cosine distance between Fmem and Fnon-mem at trimap-unknown pixels) and correlate with trimap errors on transparent objects? - Evaluate alternatives: (a) a separate memory bank for trimap decoding, (b) a light “unwind” adapter before the trimap path, (c) a regularizer discouraging foreground bias in trimap predictions. A brief ablation would validate that memory separation, not just extra capacity, is key. - In Table 5, could you add a “no weight sharing” variant of the second pass to disentangle capacity vs separation effects? 5. PDD design choices - Section 3.4 (Figure 2b) adds a mask-augmented feature concatenated with segmentation/trimap features. Have you tried stronger conditioning (e.g., cross-attention from mask features, FiLM-style affine modulation, or gating)? A small ablation would better position PDD relative to SEMatte’s parallel head. - Please detail the mask augment feature (channels, normalization, where injected) and its sensitivity. Does adding a consistency loss between mask and trimap help/hurt? 6. Trimap supervision realism - Appendix A.2 generates trimaps via morphology on image alphas. For transparent objects with spatially wide unknowns, could you compare different synthesis schemes (variable-width unknown bands, opacity-aware unknown assignment)? Any improvement when including a small set of hand-crafted trimaps for transparent cases in Stage 1? - How does Stage 2 (image-only matter training, Eq. 2) affect temporal stability? Please report dtSSD change when toggling Stage 2, and a variant where Stage 2 includes a small amount of video matting supervision. 7. Segmentation quality of M - Since PDD jointly predicts M and T (Eq. 5), please report mask metrics (IoU/F-score) on any dataset with available masks (e.g., VOS data used in training or portrait masks derived from alphas on YoutubeMatte). This will confirm that improving trimaps did not degrade mask quality. 8. Automatic mode beyond portraits - Table 3 evaluates automatic mode only on human datasets. Could you add an automatic setting on NOVM (e.g., first-frame box from a generic detector/objectness heuristic) to substantiate the “matting anything” claim? 9. Multi-object capability and memory scaling - As SAM2 supports multiple prompts, can MAM2 track/matte multiple instances simultaneously? If so, please clarify memory interaction between instances and show a small evaluation or qualitative results on multi-object sequences. How does MSS scale with K objects? 10. Efficiency reporting and apples-to-apples comparison - Table 10 counts auxiliaries for baselines but not for a “mask-provided” scenario. Please add standardized measurements where all methods receive an oracle first-frame mask (no auxiliaries), and another where all use the same interactive segmenter (e.g., SAM2-Base) for the first frame. A per-module latency/memory breakdown would enable fairer comparisons. 11. Training mixture sensitivity - Table 7 fixes equal sampling probabilities across data types. How sensitive are results to these probabilities? A brief sweep (e.g., upweighting VM or IM) would clarify robustness. Also, specify the morphological kernel ranges used to synthesize trimaps in Stage 1. 12. Backend generality of the “lightweight matter” - Section 3.1 claims compatibility with “various architectures,” but experiments use MEMatte (Appendix A.1). Could you swap in an alternative trimap-based matter and report NOVM/YoutubeMatte results to substantiate portability? 13. Metric implementation details - Please provide exact implementations/settings for MAD, MSE, Grad, and dtSSD used in Tables 2–3 (resolution, normalization, temporal window for dtSSD). This aids replication and fair comparison. 14. Long-video behavior - Given the reliance on SAM2 memory (Section 3.3) and the need to cache Fnon-mem for MSS, how do accuracy and memory behave on long/high-resolution sequences (e.g., >1k frames)? Any drift or cache eviction strategy? A plot of dtSSD vs. sequence length would be helpful. 15. Interactive corrections mid-sequence - If a user adds corrective prompts after the first frame (supported by SAM2), how does MSS/PDD incorporate them? A short study could demonstrate practical robustness in real editing workflows (Figures 11–12).
