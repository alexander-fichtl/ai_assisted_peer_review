Summary:
The paper introduces LLM-CARe, a stage-wise, multi-agent cyclic refinement framework for synthesizing EMR corpora that enforces clinical quality via three levels—corpus, section, and document—using a Critic, Adviser, and Reviser at each stage (Section 3; Figure 3). Quality is operationalized through five principles: demographic typicality, knowledge coverage, content completeness, medical correctness, and context consistency (Figure 2; Tables 6–9). On a large de-identified dataset of 192k records across 302 diseases, LLM-CARe generates EMRs without accessing real EMR text for prompting or training, yet achieves higher intrinsic quality than strong baselines (Table 1; Figure 5) and superior downstream performance on diagnosis prediction, examination recommendation, and treatment recommendation (Table 2; Figure 6). The stage-wise trajectory shows principled gains aligned with each stage’s objective (Figure 7), and ablations confirm all three agents are necessary (Figure 8). The approach is robust across different LLM backbones (Table 3) and shows low privacy risk via membership inference (Table 11). Reliability of the LLM-as-judge is supported by high agreement with clinicians and cross-model consistency (Table 4; Table 10).

Strengths:
1. Clear, principled decomposition of EMR “quality” into operational, checkable criteria across three granularities—corpus, section, document—rather than vague heuristics (Figure 2; Tables 6–9). This explicit rubric (e.g., CC onset time, Dx–PD gender validity) is a concrete methodological advance that enables targeted refinement and rigorous evaluation.

2. Novel multi-agent, stage-wise cyclic refinement (Critic–Adviser–Reviser) that adapts agent roles per stage and orders stages from “soft” distributional alignment to “strict” cross-section consistency (Section 3; Figure 3; Section 3.5). The staged ordering is justified and empirically validated by stage-aligned gains (Figure 7).

3. Corpus-level alignment handled with non-LLM critics/advisers using distributional statistics, reducing cost and leakage while preserving principled control over demographic typicality and knowledge coverage (Section 3.2; E.1 “Corpus-Level Agents”). This is a thoughtful design choice often missing in prior LLM-only pipelines.

4. Strong evidence that the framework—not mere single-pass prompting—drives the improvements: Self-Refine underperforms LLM-CARe across all metrics (Table 1; Table 2), and ablations show all three agents are necessary, with the sharpest drops when removing Critic or Reviser (Figure 8).

5. Comprehensive, multi-axis evaluation that connects intrinsic quality to utility: (i) consistent top scores on completeness, correctness, and consistency (Table 1; Figure 5); (ii) corpus-level distributional matching and coverage (Table 1); and (iii) superior downstream performance on three clinically meaningful tasks (diagnosis, exam, treatment) with reduced per-disease variance (Table 2; Figure 6).

6. Robustness of claims via multiple independent checks: cross-LLM judges with consistent rankings (Table 10), high clinician–LLM agreement with tight CIs (Table 4; Appendix L), backbone-agnostic gains (Table 3), sensitivity tests for prompt style and initial draft quality (Tables 14–15), and validation on an external dataset with stylistic shift (Table 13).

7. Privacy-preserving by design: no use of real EMR text for training or prompting, yet achieving state-of-the-art quality and utility; membership inference shows no elevated leakage compared to baselines that do not use in-context EMR text, and lower risk than a method that does (MedSyn) (Table 11). The “with real example” variant yields similar performance (Figure 9), underscoring that quality arises from the refinement framework, not memorization.

8. Fairness-oriented analyses: stratified results across gender, age brackets, and disease-frequency strata show strong performance without amplifying subgroup disparities (Figure 12 for intrinsic quality; Figure 13 for downstream tasks).

9. Reproducibility and transparency: concrete prompts for each agent and stage, JSON-structured I/O, and explicit hyperparameters/iterations (two cycles per stage) are provided (Appendix E). The evaluation metrics for demographics (TVD/Wasserstein transforms) and coverage (exact-match protocol) are clearly specified (Appendix D).

10. Practical feasibility: computational cost and scaling characteristics are quantified, with mitigation strategies (short agent outputs, early bypass when criteria are met, easy parallelization across diseases) making the approach realistic for sizable corpora (Appendix M).

11. Insightful diagnostics: fine-grained criteria breakdown (Figure 5; Figure 14) reveals where baseline methods fail (e.g., hospital course completeness for MedSyn), and case studies illustrate concrete pathology in naive generations versus progressive fixes by LLM-CARe (Table 5; Figure 10). This strengthens interpretability and guides future improvements.

12. Broad clinical relevance: tasks are carefully constructed from EMRs (Figure 4; Appendix B.2), spanning key decision points (diagnoses, exams, treatments). The method’s stronger gains on exam/treatment recommendation link back to improved knowledge coverage and corpus design (Section 5.2), providing a coherent efficacy narrative.

Weaknesses:
1. Dependence on aggregate real-EMR distributions for corpus control. Section 3.2 explicitly derives the target T_d from “aggregate statistics on the training set, including age and gender distributions and the frequencies of clinical concepts of interest.” While no raw text is used, this still requires dataset-specific distributions that may be unavailable or policy-restricted and could encode existing institutional biases. Actionable: (a) add an ablation where T_d is sourced from public epidemiology/guideline priors or noisy/shifted estimates to quantify robustness; (b) report performance when T_d is coarsened (e.g., only age/gender, without concept frequencies).

2. Metric–target coupling at the corpus level risks inflating “knowledge coverage.” The corpus critic optimizes toward concept frequency targets (Section 3.2), and coverage is then evaluated against entities extracted from the same real corpus (Appendix D; Table 9). This tight coupling makes it easier to optimize the evaluation metric rather than true clinical diversity. Actionable: hold out a subset of entities per diagnosis for evaluation-only; additionally report coverage against an independent ontology/list not used to set T_d, or via clinician audit on a stratified subset.

3. Exact string matching for coverage is brittle and potentially biased. Appendix D measures coverage via exact matches, penalizing legitimate lexical variants/synonyms and incentivizing surface-form mimicry. Actionable: add a concept-normalization step (e.g., canonical mapping or synonym tables) and report precision/recall of coverage on a manually adjudicated sample to calibrate metric validity.

4. LLM-as-judge remains a primary arbiter with a small human study. Although clinician agreement is high (Table 4; Appendix L), only 200 EMRs across 302 diseases were reviewed, with binary questions. This sample is small for broad claims and may miss nuanced clinical errors. Actionable: expand clinician evaluation with (a) per-disease stratification, (b) graded scales (minor/major error severity), and (c) targeted audits of failure modes highlighted in Figure 14.

5. Downstream task design may favor surface-form cues and lacks upper-bound context. The tasks are multiple-choice questions constructed from the same sections used in generation (Figure 4; Appendix B.2), which can introduce formatting artifacts. There is also no “real-to-real” upper bound to contextualize the performance of synthetic training. Actionable: (a) include a free-form (generation) variant or structured code prediction (e.g., diagnosis codes) to reduce format leakage; (b) report an oracle bound trained on real EMR text within the secure environment to quantify the remaining gap; (c) provide data-efficiency curves vs. synthetic corpus size.

6. Limited section coverage narrows generality. The pipeline targets seven sections (Appendix E.1), omitting common fields such as past medical history, allergies, review of systems, physical exam, and structured labs/imaging reports. This restricts applicability across clinical documentation styles. Actionable: extend Section-level CARe to additional sections with new completeness criteria (Table 6 style), and report their impact on exam/treatment tasks.

7. Temporal coherence is not enforced or measured. Document-level criteria (Tables 7–8) ensure cross-section consistency but do not check temporal/logical progression within the Hospital Course (e.g., tests before treatments, improvement before discharge). Actionable: add timeline constraints (order/causality checks) and a temporal-consistency score; quantify fixes introduced by Document-level CARe for temporal violations.

8. Bias perpetuation via demographic typicality. Aligning to in-distribution age/gender (Appendix A.4) may replicate historical biases. The subgroup analyses (Figures 12–13) show robustness, but do not test de-biasing. Actionable: run a counterfactual experiment where T_d is reweighted to a balanced demographic prior; report impacts on intrinsic quality and parity (e.g., per-subgroup accuracy on downstream tasks).

9. Limited cross-institution generalization on all tasks. External validation is provided only for diagnosis prediction (Table 13). Examination and treatment recommendation—which benefit most from corpus-level knowledge (Section 5.2)—are not stress-tested under institutional/style shift. Actionable: evaluate exam/treatment tasks on an external notes corpus or a held-out institution to validate generalization of knowledge coverage beyond source distributions.

10. Reproducibility hinges on non-shareable data. While code will be released, replication of corpus-level targets and coverage metrics requires proprietary distributions and entity lists extracted from real EMRs (Appendix D). Actionable: release (a) the full synthetic corpora used for main tables, (b) the exact agent prompts and seeds (Appendix E.1), and (c) anonymized templates or scripts that compute T_d from any private corpus so others can reproduce the pipeline in their settings.

11. Efficiency–quality tradeoffs are asserted but not quantified. Appendix M reports ~3× runtime vs direct generation and states “negligible improvements” beyond two cycles (Appendix E.3) without evidence curves. Actionable: provide Pareto curves (cycles vs. intrinsic/ downstream metrics), early-exit statistics (fraction bypassing agents per stage), and per-stage edit rates to guide practical deployment.

12. Potential pretraining contamination is unaddressed. Even without using real EMR text, backbones may memorize clinical narratives. The membership inference attack (Appendix I) only probes leakage w.r.t. the specific training split. Actionable: add a nearest-neighbor/duplication analysis against a large public clinical corpus (e.g., cross-dataset n-gram or embedding similarity) and report duplication rates for synthetic EMRs.

13. Stage interactions and regressions are not deeply analyzed. Figure 7 shows temporary fluctuations across stages, but there is no accounting of how often corpus edits induce downstream inconsistencies later corrected by Document-level CARe. Actionable: report per-stage violation counts before/after each stage, net-new vs. fixed violations, and the share of contradictions introduced by corpus edits to justify the staged ordering (Section 3.5) with quantitative evidence.

Suggestions/Questions:
1. Corpus targets and feasibility without real EMRs (Section 3.2; Eq. 2). You derive T_d “from aggregate statistics on the training set” (age, gender, concept frequencies). How would LLM-CARe perform when such distributions are unavailable or only coarsely specified? Please provide a sensitivity study that: (a) replaces T_d with public priors (e.g., age/gender by disease; no concept frequencies), (b) injects noise or shift into T_d, and (c) removes concept-frequency guidance altogether. Reporting Table 1/2 deltas under these settings would clarify deployability in data-restricted institutions.

2. Metric–target coupling for knowledge coverage (Section 3.2; Appendix D; Table 9). Because the corpus critic aligns to concept frequencies extracted from the same real data used to score coverage, the evaluation may be overly “tuned” to that list. Can you add: (a) a coverage metric computed against an independent ontology/list not used in T_d, or (b) a holdout-entity protocol where some diagnosis-specific entities are reserved for evaluation-only? This would strengthen the external validity of the “coverage” gains.

3. Exact-string matching limitations (Appendix D). Exact matches penalize legitimate synonyms (e.g., “myocardial infarction” vs “heart attack”) and reward surface-form mimicry. Could you add a normalized concept evaluation (simple synonym table or code mapping) and report precision/recall on a human-adjudicated subset? Even a small audit would calibrate the current metric.

4. Details of S(t)k selection and corpus editing policy (Eq. 3). What algorithm chooses S(t)k (greedy, importance sampling, optimization)? How do you avoid over-editing individual records or creating new cross-section inconsistencies during corpus-level revisions? Please quantify: per-iteration edit rates, record-level edit counts, and any safeguards (caps, diversity constraints).

5. Stage interactions and regressions (Section 3.5; Figure 7). Figure 7 shows temporary fluctuations across stages, but the mechanism is unclear. Can you provide per-stage violation counts (before/after each stage) for all criteria, and the fraction of new violations introduced at each stage (especially by corpus edits)? This would concretely justify the chosen “soft-to-strict” ordering.

6. Early exit and cost-efficiency (Appendix E.3; Appendix M). You fix two cycles per stage and note ~3× runtime over direct generation. Please provide Pareto curves of cycles vs. intrinsic/downstream metrics, early-exit statistics (share bypassing later agents when criteria already met), and cost per EMR by stage. These numbers would guide practical deployment.

7. Draft pool size and selection (Section 3.1; Eq. 1). How sensitive are results to the number of initial drafts n and the policy for advancing drafts between stages? Please report performance vs. n and describe any selection strategy (top-k by critic, random, ensemble merging).

8. LLM-as-judge protocol and clinician agreement (Appendix D; Table 4; Appendix L). Were clinicians blinded to the generation method? How were diseases represented in the 200-record sample across 302 diagnoses? Could you provide per-diagnosis inter-rater reliability and a severity taxonomy (minor vs major errors)? A modest expansion or stratified reporting would increase confidence that the binary LLM judgments align with nuanced clinical review.

9. Downstream MCQ construction and difficulty (Figure 4; Appendix B.2). For exam/treatment, negatives exclude items observed with the gold diagnosis, which may make some distractors obviously incompatible. Can you add a “hard negative” setting (near-miss alternatives frequently co-occurring with similar diagnoses) and report accuracy gaps? Also, please include a “real-to-real” oracle (trained on real text in the secure environment) to contextualize absolute performance.

10. External generalization beyond diagnosis (Table 13). You show cross-institution robustness for diagnosis prediction. Since examination/treatment tasks benefit most from coverage (Section 5.2), can you replicate these two tasks on an external notes corpus or a held-out institution to verify generalization of procedures/medications beyond the source distribution?

11. Temporal/logical coherence within Hospital Course (Tables 7–8 define none). Many EMR errors are temporal (e.g., interventions before diagnostic confirmation). Do you observe such issues (Table 5 examples are mostly non-temporal)? Please consider adding simple timeline constraints (test-before-treatment, improvement-before-discharge) and report their effect in Figure 7-style trajectories.

12. Demographic typicality vs fairness (Appendix A.4; Figures 12–13). Aligning to historical distributions may reproduce institutional skews. Could you run a counterfactual with reweighted/balanced age–gender priors in T_d and report intrinsic (Table 1) and per-subgroup downstream (Figure 13) impacts, including parity metrics? This would clarify if LLM-CARe can be used for de-biasing, not only matching.

13. Privacy evaluation scope (Appendix I; Table 11). Please detail the MIA setup (black/white box, attack model, calibration). Given that backbones may contain clinical narratives, can you add (a) nearest-neighbor/duplication analysis against a public clinical corpus to check for pretraining leakage, and (b) canary-style exposure tests? These would complement MIA focused on the local training split.

14. MedSyn analysis (Section 5.1; Figure 5). You infer that in-context real EMRs may propagate omissions (e.g., hospital course completeness). Could you run a controlled variant where the in-context exemplar is a high-quality, completeness-checked record, to separate exemplar quality from method design?

15. Robustness to T_d sparsity by disease. How does the corpus stage behave for rare diagnoses where concept frequencies are poorly estimated (even after your ≥500-record filter in Appendix B.1)? Please show performance vs. diagnosis frequency bins for knowledge coverage and downstream tasks (complementing Figures 12–13).

16. Section coverage and extensibility (Appendix E.1 prompts; Table 6). The pipeline targets seven sections. Do you have preliminary criteria for commonly used sections (past medical history, allergies, review of systems, physical exam)? Even a small-scale experiment showing incremental gains on exam/treatment tasks would strengthen generality claims.

17. Backbone-agnostic generation details (Table 3). Were decoding hyperparameters identical across LLaMA/Meditron/R1? Any failure modes unique to specific backbones (hallucinated meds, formatting drift)? Brief qualitative error profiles would help practitioners pick backbones.

18. Reproducibility artifacts. To facilitate adoption without real EMR access, could you release: (a) the full synthetic corpora used for Tables 1–2, (b) scripts to compute T_d from any private corpus, and (c) the entity lists (or hashed versions) and evaluation scripts for coverage? This would allow realistic end-to-end replication.

19. Failure analysis beyond Figure 10. A confusion matrix of the most frequent violations per criterion (Figure 14 set), before/after each stage, would make it easier to see where the framework still struggles and where to focus future criteria (e.g., medication dosing specifics vs. regimen duration).

20. Human-in-the-loop potential. Given the structured CARe cycles, have you explored inserting sparse clinician feedback at the document stage on a small subset (e.g., 1–2% of drafts) and propagating fixes? Reporting marginal gains per minute of expert time could make a compelling case for semi-automated curation.
