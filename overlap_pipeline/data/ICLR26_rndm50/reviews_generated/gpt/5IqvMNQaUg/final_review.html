Q1 (Summary):
Briefly summarize the paper and its contributions. Do not critique the paper here. The authors should agree with a well-written summary. Typical range: 3-8 sentences.
The paper introduces SOPS, a scalable communication scheme for cooperative MARL that separates global reachability from task-adaptive selection by using an exponential graph as a small‑diameter backbone and learning a sparse, time‑varying subgraph via a pairwise scoring network with edge‑type embeddings (Sec. 4; Fig. 2; Eq. 1–2). Discrete edge choices are enabled by Gumbel‑sigmoid reparameterization with linear temperature annealing, yielding hard links at inference while remaining end‑to‑end differentiable during training (Sec. 4.1; Eq. 3–4). Selected neighbor messages are aggregated with lightweight cross‑attention and grounded by auxiliary objectives—global‑state prediction or contrastive InfoNCE—combined with standard CTDE learners such as QMIX (Sec. 4.2–4.3; Eq. 5–9). On large‑scale benchmarks (MAgent Battle/AdversarialPursuit and IMP), SOPS reports higher returns/win rates and faster convergence than VDN, GACG, and ExpoComm variants, and boosts multiple value‑decomposition backbones when plugged in (Fig. 3; Tab. 1; Tab. 2). The method demonstrates zero‑shot transfer from smaller to larger populations while maintaining sparse degree and high multi‑hop coverage, with frequent edges persisting across scales (Fig. 4; Tab. 6–7). Ablations highlight the importance of linear annealing and reparameterized gradients, and profiling shows bounded bandwidth with moderate compute/memory overhead as agent count grows (Sec. 5.4; Fig. 5; Tab. 8).
Q2 (Soundness):
Please assign the paper a numerical rating on the following scale to indicate the soundness of the technical claims, experimental and research methodology and on whether the central claims of the paper are adequately supported with evidence: 4 excellent, 3 good, 2 fair, 1 poor.
The methodology is clearly specified and technically standard: exponential-backbone connectivity (Eq. 1), differentiable pairwise scoring with Gumbel-sigmoid and straight-through estimator (Eq. 2–4), cross-attention aggregation (Eq. 5), and well-defined training objectives with auxiliary grounding (Eq. 6–9) in a CTDE setup (Sec. 4). Claims of scalability, efficiency, and adaptability are supported by multi-scenario evidence: consistent gains over strong baselines on MAgent and IMP (Fig. 3; Tab. 1), plug-and-play improvements across value-decomposition learners (Tab. 2), robust zero-shot transfer to larger populations (Fig. 4), and diagnostics showing sparse degree with high multi-hop coverage and edge persistence (Tab. 6–7). Ablations substantiate key design choices (linear annealing and reparameterized gradients; Fig. 5), and system profiling corroborates bounded bandwidth and moderate overhead (Tab. 8). Minor weaknesses include small seed counts (3), limited domains, exclusion of GACG from transfer (architectural mismatch), and no formal theory beyond coverage analyses (Fig. 1). Overall, the central claims are well supported empirically and the methodology is sound. Rating: 3

Q5 (Strengths):
Please give a substantive assessment of the strengths of the paper. Be specific, avoid generic remarks. This can, for example, include quality, clarity (e.g. well written), or significance, originality (e.g. a new definition or problem formulation, creative combinations of existing ideas, application to a new domain, or removing limitations from prior results.) 1. Clear, original decomposition of the communication design space. The paper “separates global reachability from task‑adaptive selection” by pairing a bounded‑bandwidth exponential backbone with learned sparse, time‑varying edges (Sec. 4; Fig. 2; Eq. 1–2). This addresses two long‑standing, often conflated challenges—scalability (small diameter, near‑linear cost) and adaptability (task‑driven links)—in a way that neither fixed topologies (e.g., EC‑S) nor dense, learned graphs (e.g., GACG) achieve. 2. Strong principled justification for the backbone choice. The broadcast‑coverage study quantifies multi‑round dissemination under a unified per‑round bandwidth and shows a consistent advantage of exponential graphs over Erdős–Rényi and Torus across scales (Fig. 1). This gives a concrete, scale‑sensitive rationale for the skeleton beyond intuition. 3. Practically effective, end‑to‑end differentiable edge sampling. The Gumbel‑sigmoid with straight‑through estimator and linear temperature annealing (Eq. 3–4) turns discrete topology selection into a stable optimization component. The ablations demonstrate that (i) linear cooling outperforms exponential/constant schedules (Fig. 5a) and (ii) reparameterized gradients outperform SFE and ARM in both peak performance and stability (Fig. 5c). This is a careful, design‑level contribution seldom isolated so clearly in MARL comms papers. 4. Budget‑aware sparsity with proven multi‑hop reachability. Pairwise scoring is restricted to backbone candidates (Eq. 2), avoiding O(N^2) costs while still achieving high multi‑hop coverage (Tab. 6). The method keeps realized out‑degree small (≈3–4.6) yet maintains fast information propagation, aligning efficiency with effectiveness. 5. Strong, consistent empirical gains at scale. Across MAgent (Battle, AdversarialPursuit) and IMP, SOPS learns faster and achieves higher returns/win rates than VDN, GACG, and ExpoComm variants (Fig. 3; Tab. 1). AUC metrics further corroborate superior cumulative learning under identical budgets (Tab. 4–5). 6. Convincing zero‑shot resizing with structural diagnostics. SOPS transfers from smaller to larger populations with the highest win rates across all train→test pairs (Fig. 4). The authors go beyond outcomes, showing why it works: sparse degree with high coverage@3–4 and strong edge‑frequency persistence (high Pearson ρ, large Jaccard overlap) after resizing (Tab. 6–7). This combination of performance and mechanistic evidence is notably thorough. 7. Plug‑and‑play generality across CTDE learners. Adding SOPS improves QMIX, QPLEX, and SHAQ on IMP, with the best/second‑best in every scenario being a “+ SOPS” variant (Tab. 2). This indicates the contribution is orthogonal to—and beneficial for—diverse value‑decomposition backbones. 8. Sensible auxiliary grounding that stabilizes communication learning. The use of lightweight state‑prediction or contrastive InfoNCE (Eq. 6–7) adds minimal complexity while making messages meaningfully informative, addressing brittleness reported in prior graph‑based comms (Sec. 4.3). 9. System‑level profiling that matches the scalability claim. The paper reports step/s, GPU memory, activation size, and realized degree across N (Tab. 8). SOPS maintains moderate overhead and bounded degree as N increases, contrasting sharply with GACG’s quadratic blow‑up—an unusually transparent, decision‑relevant systems view. 10. Reproducibility and clarity of the technical core. The algorithmic components are precisely specified (Eq. 1–9), the architecture is easy to follow (Fig. 2), and hyperparameters/common settings are centralized (Appendix C.1; Tab. 3). Code is provided (Reproducibility Statement), facilitating verification and reuse. 11. Careful ablations on non‑obvious knobs. Beyond temperatures and gradient estimators, the edge‑budget study (Fig. 5b) isolates the benefit of adaptive neighbor selection over static K‑NN on the exponential backbone, strengthening the case that the learned selection—not just the backbone—drives the gains.
Q6 (Weaknesses):
Please give a substantive assessment of the weaknesses of the paper. Focus on constructive and actionable insights on how the work could improve towards its stated goals. Be specific, avoid generic remarks. For example, if you believe the contribution lacks novelty, provide references and an explanation as evidence; if you believe experiments are insufficient, explain why and exactly what is missing, etc. Unclarities are adressed later, so only list real weaknesses. If you have many, only list the least generic ones.
1. Limited baseline coverage for “large-scale” comms approximations - Evidence: Related work cites mean-field and attention approximations (GAT‑MF, mix‑attention; Sec. 2), but experiments only compare to VDN, GACG, and ExpoComm (Sec. 5.1). - Why it matters: Claims about scalability/adaptability would be stronger if contrasted with recent scalable approximations that avoid all‑to‑all while remaining learnable. - Actionable: Add baselines adapted to the same bandwidth budget K: (i) mean‑field (e.g., GAT‑MF) with bounded neighbor sampling; (ii) attention with top‑k masking or entmax/sparsemax; (iii) GTDE (grouped training) using the same per‑round edge budget. Report step/s, GPU memory, realized degree, and performance (as in Tab. 8). 2. Scalability not stress‑tested beyond 121 agents in RL - Evidence: Broadcast coverage in Fig. 1 explores up to 1024 nodes, but RL evaluations peak at N=121 (Fig. 3; Tab. 8). - Why it matters: The central claim is near‑linear, scalable communication; empirical end‑to‑end evidence at larger N is missing. - Actionable: Extend Battle and AdversarialPursuit to N≈256–512 with identical budgets and report: returns/win‑rates, AUC (Tabs. 4–5 style), throughput/memory/activation (Tab. 8), realized out‑degree distributions, and zero‑shot transfer 100→256. Even partial runs (lower training steps) can reveal scaling trends. 3. Potential auxiliary‑loss advantage and fairness across methods - Evidence: Sec. 4.3 and C.1: SOPS and ExpoComm variants use auxiliary grounding with shared α, κ, M; it’s unclear whether GACG/VDN receive equivalent auxiliary signals. - Why it matters: If only some methods benefit from state‑prediction or InfoNCE under CTDE, comparisons may conflate the effect of the communication scheme with that of extra supervision. - Actionable: (i) Add “+Aux” variants for all baselines (including GACG/VDN) using the same predictor/contrastive head and report deltas; (ii) provide ablations “SOPS w/o Aux” on all scenarios to quantify dependence on auxiliary losses. 4. Bandwidth control is probabilistic, not hard‑constrained - Evidence: Edges are sampled with independent Bernoulli gates (Eq. 2–4), and hard inference is I(ℓij>0), without an explicit per‑node top‑k constraint; realized degrees vary (Tab. 8; Tab. 6). - Why it matters: For strict budgeted systems, stochastic edge counts can exceed quota and complicate deployment guarantees. - Actionable: Evaluate Gumbel‑Top‑k (or Gumbel‑Softmax with top‑k masking) per sender to enforce a hard K and compare stability, performance, and degree variance to the Bernoulli/ST estimator. Report per‑step budget violations for the current SOPS. 5. Backbone choice not causally tied to RL outcomes - Evidence: Fig. 1 shows broadcast coverage advantages of exponential graphs vs. ER/Torus under synthetic diffusion, but RL results only use the exponential backbone (Sec. 5.2). - Why it matters: It remains unclear whether the downstream gains stem from the backbone or the learned pairwise selection. - Actionable: Keep the SOPS scorer fixed and swap backbones (ER with same degree; Torus) in Battle/APurs. Report performance, AUC, edge persistence (Tab. 7 metrics), and coverage@h (Tab. 6) to quantify the backbone’s causal role in RL. 6. Insufficient statistical power - Evidence: “All experiments are averaged over three random seeds” (Sec. 5.1); CIs are shown but no significance tests; some curves overlap (Fig. 3). - Why it matters: With 3 seeds, differences—especially among top methods—may not be statistically reliable. - Actionable: Increase seeds to 5–10 for main scenarios, report bootstrapped CIs on AUC (Tabs. 4–5) and paired tests (e.g., Wilcoxon or permutation) on final performance and AUC. Include effect sizes. 7. Robustness claims not validated experimentally - Evidence: Sec. C.3.2 acknowledges no targeted agent/edge failures; robustness is inferred from coverage patterns and resizing transfer. - Why it matters: Real deployments face node/edge dropouts and congestion. Demonstrating resilience is core to scalability claims. - Actionable: Add tests with random and structured (contiguous ring segment) node/edge dropouts at test time; also inject message loss/noise. Report performance degradation, coverage@h, and edge‑reallocation dynamics under SOPS vs. ExpoComm. 8. Communication cost reported as degree, not payload - Evidence: System profiling (Tab. 8) reports realized out‑degree, step/s, GPU mem, activations, but not bits/step or message dimension sensitivity. - Why it matters: Practical bandwidth is function of edges × message size; tuning message size trades off accuracy vs. bandwidth. - Actionable: Report average per‑step transmitted bytes (edges × message dim × precision), and ablate message dimension and quantization (e.g., 8‑bit vs. 16‑bit) on performance and throughput. 9. Centralized scorer during training may hide decentralized constraints - Evidence: “In our CTDE implementation these scores are computed centrally on the learner from the full tensor Zt” (Sec. 4.1). - Why it matters: Realistic decentralized execution may not have instantaneous access to all Zt; training with fully centralized scorer can overestimate practicality. - Actionable: Implement and evaluate a decentralized scorer that uses only local features and backbone‑reachable messages within h hops; compare performance and overhead to centralized scoring. Alternatively, throttle centralized information during training to match an assumed communication latency/budget. 10. Limited analysis of the message aggregation design - Evidence: Aggregation uses “lightweight scaled cross‑attention” (Sec. 4.2), but there is no ablation against simple sum/mean, GRU pooling, or multi‑round per‑timestep aggregation depth. - Why it matters: Gains could partially stem from the aggregator rather than topology selection; multi‑round aggregation per step may improve multi‑hop fusion under the same budget. - Actionable: Ablate aggregator choices and number of attention heads/layers; test one‑shot vs. multi‑round aggregation per environment step under a fixed bandwidth. Report compute/memory trade‑offs. 11. Generalization limited to homogeneous or weakly heterogeneous agents - Evidence: Sec. C.3.3 acknowledges focus on homogeneous/weak heterogeneity; claims of type‑awareness via embeddings are not empirically validated. - Why it matters: Many large‑scale systems are strongly heterogeneous (roles/capabilities). - Actionable: Add heterogeneous variants of IMP (e.g., varying cost/risk models per agent type) and include explicit type embeddings eij in Eq. 2; ablate the contribution of edge‑type embeddings by removing/perturbing them. 12. One‑way resizing and no dynamic population changes - Evidence: Zero‑shot transfer evaluates small→large (Fig. 4); no large→small or dynamic mid‑episode changes. - Why it matters: Real systems often shrink/expand or suffer churn. - Actionable: Evaluate large→small transfer (e.g., 121→64) and dynamic resizing during episodes; measure recovery time, edge persistence (Tab. 7), and coverage@h (Tab. 6) across transitions. Collectively, addressing these points—broader baselines under matched budgets, larger‑N RL stress tests, stricter bandwidth control, robustness experiments, payload accounting, decentralized scorer variants, aggregator ablations, and richer generalization tests—would substantially strengthen the evidence behind SOPS’s scalability and adaptability claims while making the methodology more deployable under realistic constraints.
